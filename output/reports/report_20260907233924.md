# Basic Linear Regression: From Correlation to Diagnostics and CAPM

## Executive Summary
This chapter develops the one-explanatory-variable case as a complete introduction to regression reasoning. It moves from scatterplots and correlation through least-squares fitting, population assumptions, variability decomposition, standard errors, t tests, confidence and prediction intervals, residual diagnostics, unusual points, the CAPM financial application, and a matrix-algebra bridge to multiple regression. The Wisconsin Lottery example supplies concrete numerical results throughout, while the Lincoln National CAPM example shows how statistical significance and explanatory power can differ.

## From relationship to fitted line
The chapter begins with scatterplots and Pearson correlation as summaries of a two-variable relationship, then moves to least squares so the relationship can be stated as a reproducible fitted line. In the Wisconsin Lottery illustration, population and sales over 50 ZIP-code areas have r=0.886, yielding the fitted line yhat=469.7+0.647x. Correlation is dimensionless and invariant to location and scale changes, but only measures linear association; the source’s y=x^2 example has zero correlation despite a perfect nonlinear relationship.

- Pearson r is bounded between -1 and 1 and measures linear relationship.

## Population model and coefficient precision
The basic model is presented equivalently through observable conditional moments and an error representation: E(y_i)=beta0+beta1x_i or y_i=beta0+beta1x_i+epsilon_i. It assumes fixed x values, common response variance, and independent responses; normality is often an added assumption for exact t-based inference. Least-squares coefficients are weighted sums of responses, making unusual observations consequential. The slope is unbiased, and its standard error declines with more observations, smaller residual spread, and a wider spread of explanatory values.

### Key Claims
- The observables and error representations F1-F4 and E1-E4 are equivalent.
  Sources: [Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 2 Basic Linear Regression.tex:population-model-and-coefficient-precision]
- The slope standard error is s/[s_x sqrt(n-1)].
  Sources: [Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 2 Basic Linear Regression.tex:population-model-and-coefficient-precision]

## Fit, inference, and prediction
The chapter partitions total response variability into Error SS and Regression SS, defining R-squared as the explained proportion. It estimates error variance with MSE=Error SS/(n-2), calling its square root residual standard deviation. For the lottery model, R-squared is 78.5% and the slope t statistic is 13.4, exceeding the two-sided 5% critical value 2.011 with 48 degrees of freedom. The 95% slope interval is (0.549,0.745); a POP=10,000 ZIP code has predicted sales 6,939.7 with a much wider prediction interval because an individual new response also varies around its mean.

### Key Claims
- In one-variable regression, R-squared equals r-squared.
  Sources: [Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 2 Basic Linear Regression.tex:fit,-inference,-and-prediction]
- For POP=10,000, the reported point prediction is 6,939.7.
  Sources: [Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 2 Basic Linear Regression.tex:fit,-inference,-and-prediction]

## Diagnostics and unusual observations
Residual analysis treats observed residuals as proxies for unobserved errors and asks whether residuals display patterns that suggest a better specification. The chapter lists lack of independence, heteroscedasticity, relations between errors and explanatory variables, nonnormal errors, and unusual points. An outlier is vertically unusual; a high-leverage point is horizontally unusual; either can change a fit. The Kenosha lottery observation illustrates why deletion should not be automatic: removing it raises R-squared from 78.5% to 88.3%, but its unusual sales have a substantive explanation involving Illinois-border participation.

### Key Claims
- A standardized residual exceeding two in absolute value is considered unusually large in the source.
  Sources: [Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 2 Basic Linear Regression.tex:diagnostics-and-unusual-observations]
- Kenosha’s removal increases reported R-squared from 78.5% to 88.3%.

## Financial application and matrix extension
The CAPM illustration models Lincoln National monthly return using an equally weighted S&P 500 market return over 1986-1990. The fitted slope of 0.973 is statistically important, but R-squared of 35.4% shows the market is only a partial explanation. The October 1987 market crash is a high-leverage point; removing it reduces R-squared to 26.4%, underscoring that leverage can expand the observed range of a relationship as well as distort it. The technical supplement then introduces matrices, vectors, transposes, products, inverses, and variance-covariance matrices, culminating in y=X beta+epsilon and the broader least-squares representation.

## Conclusions
Basic linear regression is not merely a line-drawing device. It is a linked system of relationship summaries, model assumptions, fitted values, uncertainty measures, and diagnostics. The chapter’s repeated examples show why practitioners need all of those pieces: a strong t statistic can coexist with only partial explanatory power, and an unusual observation can impair a fit, reveal an error, or carry the most important substantive story in the data.
