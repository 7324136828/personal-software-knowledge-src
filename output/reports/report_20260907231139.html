Regression and the Normal Distribution: An Actuarial Foundation

Executive Summary

This chapter excerpt from Frees' 'Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications' introduces regression analysis as the statistical method most influential across actuarial and financial fields, grounding it historically in Galton's 1885 study of parent-child heights and theoretically in the normal distribution. It develops the summary statistics and normal-curve machinery (mean, median, standard deviation, percentiles, qq plots) needed to assess normality, introduces the Box-Cox family of power transforms (especially the logarithm) for symmetrizing right-skewed data typical of insurance claims, formalizes the sampling assumptions underlying inference, distinguishes designed/experimental from observational data, and connects all of this to the three traditional actuarial application areas of pricing, reserving, and solvency testing, closing with a technical supplement on the Central Limit Theorem and the Edgeworth approximation.

Regression as the Central Statistical Method

The chapter opens by situating regression within the broader discipline of statistics, defined as the collection, summarization, and analysis of data to make statements about the real world. Among many applied statistical methods (sample surveys, experimental design, process control, forecasting), regression analysis is singled out as the most influential, illustrated by the fact that more than 24,000 articles applying regression appeared in the ABI/INFORM index of business journals between 1978 and 2007. The historical origin of regression is traced to Sir Francis Galton's 1885 presidential address, in which he studied the joint distribution of parents' and adult children's heights and linked his findings to normal curve theory.

Regression analysis's distinguishing feature is its ability to make statements about a variable of interest after controlling for known explanatory variables.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.1]

The Normal Curve and Summary Statistics

Section 1.2 formalizes the normal distribution as f(y) = (1/(sigma*sqrt(2*pi))) * exp(-(y-mu)^2/(2*sigma^2)), governed by a location parameter mu and a scale parameter sigma^2. The chapter grounds this in the Massachusetts automobile bodily injury claims example (n=272, from Rempala and Derrig, 2005), defining the core summary statistics used throughout the book: the mean, median, standard deviation, and percentile. Visual diagnostics — histograms, box plots, and quantile-quantile (qq) plots — are introduced as tools for assessing whether a sample distribution is well approximated by the normal curve, revealing in this example a mild left skew after data are already expressed in log-thousands of dollars.

Power Transforms for Skewed Data

Section 1.3 introduces the Box-Cox family of power transforms, y^(lambda) = (y^lambda - 1)/lambda for lambda != 0 and ln(y) for lambda = 0, as a general tool for symmetrizing skewed distributions. Because many actuarial variables (insurance claims, wages, asset values, housing prices) are right-skewed, log transforms are especially common. The point is reinforced with a simulation of 500 chi-square(2) observations, comparing the raw distribution against square-root, logarithmic, and negative-reciprocal transforms, with the logarithmic transform providing the best approximation to symmetry in that example.

The Box-Cox transform reduces to the natural logarithm ln(y) specifically when lambda = 0.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.3]
Many insurance claims distributions are right-skewed (positively skewed), with a longer tail on the right and mass concentrated to the left.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.3]

Sampling Assumptions and Study Design

Section 1.4 lays out four Basic Sampling Assumptions underlying formal statistical inference: the mean E(y_i)=mu, variance Var(y_i)=sigma^2, independence of observations, and normality of the y_i. The text is explicit that the normality assumption is dispensable for many inference procedures because central limit theorems supply approximate normality for many statistics regardless of the parent distribution's shape. Section 1.5 then distinguishes designed (experimental) studies, where the analyst controls data collection (illustrated by the RAND Health Insurance Experiment's random assignment of 14,insurance plans across six cities), from observational studies, where analysts instead rely on statistical control for confounding variables, as in the Galton height data where family diet could confound the parent-child height relationship.

The four Basic Sampling Assumptions are E(y_i)=mu, Var(y_i)=sigma^2, independence of {y_i}, and normality of {y_i}.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.4]

Actuarial Applications: Pricing, Reserving, Solvency

Section 1.6 ties the statistical machinery to the three traditional areas of actuarial application: pricing, reserving, and solvency testing. In pricing, regression-based classification systems allow insurers to price risk according to explanatory variables (sex, age, location, vehicle purpose, etc.), avoiding the adverse-selection spiral that results from flat-rate pricing, in which good risks leave for cheaper competitors while bad risks remain and drive rates upward. Reserving uses regression to forecast future obligations, while solvency testing assesses whether capital is adequate to meet those obligations, sometimes by comparing healthy and financially distressed firms.

Flat-rate insurance pricing that ignores explanatory variables leads to adverse selection, in which good risks leave and bad risks remain, forcing rates upward.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.6]
The three traditional areas of actuarial application discussed are pricing, reserving, and solvency testing.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.6]

Technical Supplement: The Central Limit Theorem

Section 1.9 provides a formal statement of the Central Limit Theorem: for independently distributed y_1,...,y_n with mean mu, finite variance sigma^2, and finite third moment, the rescaled sample mean sqrt(n)/sigma*(ybar-mu) converges in distribution to a standard normal as n approaches infinity. The text notes that sample sizes of n=25 to 50 typically provide adequate approximations in practice. The related Edgeworth Approximation refines this by adding a skewness-correction term proportional to E(ybar-mu)^3, showing that the normal approximation is more accurate for symmetric parent populations than skewed ones — the theoretical justification for the chapter's earlier emphasis on symmetrizing transforms.

The Central Limit Theorem states that sqrt(n)/sigma*(ybar-mu) converges to a standard normal distribution as n approaches infinity, given independence, finite variance, and finite third absolute moment.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.9]
Sample sizes of n=25 to 50 are cited as generally providing adequate normal approximations in practice.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.9]
The Edgeworth Approximation adds a correction term involving the skewness E(ybar-mu)^3, indicating the normal approximation improves as skewness approaches zero.[Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex:section-1.9]

Conclusions

Taken together, this chapter excerpt builds the conceptual and technical scaffold on which the rest of the book's actuarial regression modeling rests: the normal distribution and its summary statistics provide the language for describing data, power transforms (especially the logarithm) make skewed real-world actuarial data tractable under that language, formal sampling assumptions and the Central Limit Theorem justify treating estimators as approximately normal even when raw data are not, and the designed-versus-observational distinction reminds practitioners that most actuarial data supports statistical control and association rather than strict causal inference — all of which underlie the practical work of pricing, reserving, and solvency testing that motivates the book.
