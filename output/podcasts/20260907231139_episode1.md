## Sampling Designs
**Jordan:** The book opens this section by reminding us that approximating normality is an important issue in practical regression applications. It previews the structure of the whole book here too: Parts one and two focus on linear regression, learning basic concepts and sampling design, while Part three covers nonlinear regression for binary, count, and fat-tailed responses, where the normal curve isn't the most helpful reference distribution anymore.
**Maya:** But the ideas about basic concepts and design carry over into that nonlinear setting too, so what we learn here in chapter one isn't wasted once the models get more complicated later in the book.
**Jordan:** In regression analysis generally, you focus on one measurement of interest, the dependent variable, and other measurements serve as explanatory variables. The goal is to explain differences in the dependent variable in terms of differences in those explanatory variables.
**Maya:** And because regression shows up across so many fields, researchers use a lot of different vocabulary for what's fundamentally the same y-variable-versus-x-variable setup. Table 1.3 in the book lays out a whole list of these alternative terms.
**Jordan:** Let's go through some of these pairs, because you'll definitely encounter this terminology switching depending on which field's papers you're reading. For the y-variable, you might hear it called the outcome of interest, the dependent variable, the endogenous variable, the response, the regressand, the left-hand-side variable, the explained variable, or the output.
**Maya:** And correspondingly, for the x-variable, you might hear explanatory variable, independent variable, exogenous variable, treatment, regressor, right-hand-side variable, predictor variable, or input.
**Jordan:** So "dependent variable" and "outcome" and "response" and "regressand" and "output" are all pointing at the same thing, just different disciplinary dialects. Economists might say endogenous and exogenous, experimentalists might say response and treatment, and so on.
**Maya:** Good to have that Rosetta Stone in mind before diving into papers from different fields. Now, the book gives some historical context for when statistics started making a serious impact on experimental science, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
**Jordan:** Experimental sciences often use designed studies, where the data collection itself is under the control of an analyst. These happen in laboratory settings with tight physical restrictions on variables the researcher thinks matter, but designed studies also happen in larger field experiments, where the mechanisms for control look different than in a lab.
**Maya:** Agriculture and medicine are classic domains for designed studies. And data collected from a designed study is called experimental data.
**Jordan:** The book gives a really clean illustrative example: the yield of a crop like corn, where each parcel of land, the observation, gets assigned different levels of fertilizer, the explanatory variable. The goal is to figure out the effect of fertilizer on corn yield, the response variable.
**Maya:** Researchers try to make the parcels of land as similar as possible to each other, but differences inevitably creep in anyway. That's where randomization comes in: agricultural researchers use randomization techniques to assign different fertilizer levels to different parcels.
**Jordan:** With that randomization in place, analysts can explain the variation in corn yields in terms of variation in fertilizer levels, and because of the randomized assignment, they can actually infer that the treatment has a causal effect on the response. The book points forward to chapter 6 for a deeper discussion of causality.
**Maya:** Now here's a really important real-world example of a designed study outside of agriculture: the RAND Health Insurance Experiment. This is a study reported by Manning and colleagues in 1987, designed to answer a genuinely difficult causal question about medical care and insurance demand.
**Jordan:** The underlying puzzle is this: many studies find a positive relationship between how much people spend on medical care and how much health insurance they demand. People in poor health anticipate using more medical services than similarly situated healthy people, so they seek higher levels of insurance to cover those anticipated expenditures.
**Maya:** And they can get that additional insurance in a few ways: selecting a more generous plan from their employer, choosing an employer that offers a more generous plan in the first place, or paying more out of pocket for individual coverage. The problem is that this makes it genuinely hard to disentangle cause and effect between medical spending and insurance availability using purely observational data.
**Jordan:** So the RAND study used a carefully designed experiment instead. Enrolled households from six cities, between November 1974 and February 1977, were randomly assigned to one of fourteen different insurance plans.
**Maya:** Those fourteen plans varied along a couple of cost-sharing dimensions. First, the co-insurance rate, meaning the percentage of out-of-pocket costs the household had to pay, which varied among zero percent, twenty-five percent, fifty percent, and ninety-five percent. Second, the deductible, set at five percent, ten percent, or fifteen percent of family income, up to a maximum of one thousand dollars.
**Jordan:** Because the assignment to insurance plans, and therefore to levels of the treatment, the amount of health insurance, was random, the study found that more favorable plans led to greater total expenditures, and this held even after controlling for participants' underlying health status.
**Maya:** That's a genuinely strong causal claim, and it's only possible because of the random assignment. Now contrast that with actuarial science and most other social sciences, where the book says designed studies are, quote, the exception rather than the rule.
**Jordan:** The book gives a vivid counterexample: if you wanted to study the effect of smoking on mortality, it's essentially impossible to get study participants to agree to be randomly assigned to smoker versus nonsmoker groups for years, just so researchers could observe their mortality patterns. That would be both unethical and impractical.
**Maya:** So, just like with the Galton height study from section 1.1, social science researchers generally have to work with observational data instead, meaning data that isn't under the control of the analyst.
**Jordan:** And here's the crucial limitation: with observational data, you cannot infer causal relationships in the same way, but you can still readily introduce measures of association. The book uses the Galton example again here. It's apparent that tall parents tend to have tall children, and conversely, short parents tend to have short children.
**Maya:** The book flags that chapter 2 will introduce correlation and other measures of association for exactly this purpose. But it's careful to stress that you still cannot infer causality just from that association.
**Jordan:** Why not? Because there could be another variable, like family diet, that's related to both the parents' height and the children's height. Good family diet could be associated with taller heights in both parents and children, while poor diet might stifle growth in both generations.
**Maya:** If that's the case, the book calls family diet a confounding variable, something that's related to both the explanatory variable and the response, and that could be driving the observed association instead of, or in addition to, a direct causal link.
**Jordan:** In designed experiments, like the RAND Health Insurance Experiment, you can control for the effects of variables like health status through random assignment itself, that's the whole point of randomization. But in observational studies, we instead rely on statistical control rather than experimental control.
**Maya:** To illustrate statistical control with the Galton data, you might split your observations into two groups, one for good family diet and one for poor family diet, and then examine the relationship between parents' and children's height separately within each subgroup.
**Jordan:** The book says this is really the essence of the regression method: comparing a y and an x while, quote, controlling for the effects of other explanatory variables. That's the statistical analog of the experimental control you'd get from randomization.
**Maya:** And of course, to actually do this kind of statistical control, you have to remember to record family diet and any other variables that might confound the relationship between parents' height and children's height in the first place. The book is honest that this is genuinely hard: trying to imagine all the variables that could possibly affect a response is close to an impossible task in most social science problems.
**Jordan:** To help with that challenge, the book points forward to chapter 6, which discusses measures of an explanatory variable's importance and its impact on model selection, essentially, how do you decide which variables are worth including.

## Actuarial Applications
**Jordan:** The book frames this book's whole introduction to regression around what it calls the traditional triad of statistical inference: hypothesis testing, estimation, and prediction.
**Maya:** And then it lays out the three traditional areas of actuarial application that this methodology serves: pricing, reserving, and solvency testing. Let's take these one at a time, starting with pricing and adverse selection.
**Jordan:** Regression analysis can be used to determine insurance prices across many lines of business. The book's example is private passenger automobile insurance, where expected claims vary by the insured's sex, age, location, whether it's a city or a rural area, vehicle purpose, work or pleasure, and a whole host of other explanatory variables. Regression helps identify which of those variables are actually important determinants of expected claims.
**Maya:** Here's the key economic insight, though: in competitive markets, insurance companies don't use the same price for every insured. And the book explains exactly why that would be a bad idea if they did.
**Jordan:** If a company charged one flat rate for everyone, quote, good risks, meaning people with lower-than-average expected claims, would end up overpaying relative to their actual risk, and they'd leave the company for a better deal elsewhere. Meanwhile, quote, bad risks, people with higher-than-average expected claims, would find that flat rate to be a good deal, and they'd stay.
**Maya:** If the company kept that flat-rate policy going, it would have to keep raising premiums to compensate for claims from its increasing share of bad risks, and its market share would keep dwindling as it continued losing good risks. The book gives this phenomenon a name: adverse selection.
**Jordan:** The fix, or at least the mitigation, is to use an appropriate set of explanatory variables to build classification systems, so that each insured ends up paying something closer to their fair share based on their actual risk profile, rather than a flat rate that systematically favors bad risks over good ones.
**Maya:** That's pricing. Next, reserving and solvency testing, which the book treats together because they're conceptually related. Both are concerned with predicting whether the liabilities associated with a group of policies will exceed the capital that's been set aside to meet those obligations.
**Jordan:** Reserving specifically involves determining the appropriate amount of capital needed to meet those future obligations. Solvency testing is about assessing whether the capital on hand is adequate to fund the obligations for an entire block of business.
**Maya:** In some practice areas, regression can be used to forecast future obligations directly, which helps determine appropriate reserve levels, and the book points to chapter 19 for more on that. Regression can also be used to compare the characteristics of healthy versus financially distressed firms, which is directly relevant to solvency testing, and the book points to chapter 14 for that application.
**Jordan:** Beyond those three traditional areas, the book notes that regression is a genuinely general-purpose quantitative tool, applicable to a broad variety of business problems, not just pricing, reserving, and solvency testing. By becoming familiar with regression, actuaries gain a flexible quantitative skill they can bring to bear on all sorts of problems involving the financial security of people, companies, and governmental organizations.
**Maya:** The book mentions it will illustrate a lot of these nonactuarial applications through featured vignettes it labels as, quote, examples, plus various illustrative datasets throughout the text. And it suggests that even if you don't sit down and actually complete the numerical exercises in Chapter 1, just reviewing the problem descriptions will help you get a feel for the breadth of applications where actuaries might use regression.

## Central Limit Theorem
**Maya:** The book opens by saying central limit theorems form the basis for much of the statistical inference used in regression analysis, so it's worth stating one version of the theorem explicitly rather than just gesturing at it.
**Jordan:** Here's the formal statement. Suppose y-one through y-n are independently distributed with mean mu, finite variance sigma-squared, and the expected value of the absolute value of y cubed is finite. Then, as n goes to infinity, the probability that the square root of n over sigma, times the quantity y-bar minus mu, is less than or equal to x, converges to phi of x, for every x, where phi is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.
**Maya:** In plain language: take your sample mean, subtract off the true population mean, scale it by the square root of the sample size divided by the standard deviation, and as your sample size grows, that rescaled quantity behaves more and more like a standard normal random variable, no matter what the original distribution of the y's looked like, as long as those basic conditions hold.
**Jordan:** The book adds a really practical note here too: under this theorem's assumptions, the rescaled distribution of y-bar approaches a standard normal as sample size increases, and empirical investigations have shown that sample sizes of twenty-five to fifty observations typically provide adequate approximations for most practical purposes.
**Maya:** That's a genuinely useful rule of thumb. You don't need thousands of observations for the Central Limit Theorem to start kicking in usefully, something in the twenty-five to fifty range is often already good enough.
**Jordan:** But the book also asks: when does the Central Limit Theorem not work well? And it brings in a second, more refined result to answer that: the Edgeworth Approximation.
**Maya:** The Edgeworth Approximation assumes y-one through y-n are identically and independently distributed with mean mu, finite variance sigma-squared, and again a finite third absolute moment. Under those conditions, the probability that square root of n over sigma times y-bar minus mu is less than or equal to x equals phi of x, plus a correction term, plus a remainder term that vanishes as n grows.
**Jordan:** That correction term is one-sixth times one over the square root of two pi, times e to the power negative x-squared over two, times the expected value of y minus mu cubed, all divided by sigma cubed times the square root of n. And then there's a remainder term, h-sub-n over the square root of n, where h-sub-n goes to zero as n goes to infinity.
**Maya:** The important quantity in that correction term is the expected value of y minus mu cubed, which is essentially a measure of skewness in the original population. The Edgeworth result says the distribution of y-bar gets closer to normal specifically as that skewness measure gets closer to zero.
**Jordan:** In other words, the normal approximation for y-bar is better when your underlying population is close to symmetric, and worse when the population is heavily skewed. That's genuinely important for insurance applications, because the book notes explicitly that many distributions in that domain, claims severities especially, tend to be skewed.
**Maya:** Historically, the book says, analysts actually used that second term on the right-hand side of the Edgeworth result as an explicit correction to the plain normal-curve approximation, and it points to Beard, Pentikainen, and Pesonen's 1984 book for more discussion of Edgeworth approximations specifically in actuarial science.
**Jordan:** But this book's own preferred alternative, and this ties everything in the chapter together, is the one we saw all the way back in section 1.3: transform the data first, to achieve approximate symmetry, and then rely on the ordinary Central Limit Theorem rather than needing an explicit Edgeworth correction term.
**Maya:** As the Edgeworth Approximation theorem itself suggests, if your parent population is close to symmetric to begin with, the distribution of y-bar will already be approximately normal without any extra correction needed. That's the theoretical justification for everything we did with logs and power transforms back in section 1.3.
