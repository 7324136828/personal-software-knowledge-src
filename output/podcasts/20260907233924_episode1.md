## Standardized residuals
**Maya:** Residuals are often divided by their standard errors to create standardized residuals. If responses are normal, these are approximately standard normal and values above two in absolute value are unusually large, called outliers. ## Closing Practice and Sign-off
**Maya:** One final way to organize the chapter is as a sequence of comparisons. Compare raw variables one at a time before comparing them jointly. Compare the mean-only prediction with the fitted-line prediction. Compare total variability with error variability and explained variability. Compare a confidence interval for a mean relationship with a prediction interval for a new observation. And compare a model with and without an unusual point as sensitivity analysis. Each comparison asks a distinct question.
**Jordan:** The exercises reinforce exactly that workflow. They ask students to calculate correlations, draw scatterplots, fit lines, report R squared and t statistics, test slopes against zero or one, construct confidence and prediction intervals, and examine residuals. Other exercises show that matching means, standard deviations, slopes, and R squared can conceal radically different scatterplots, using Anscombe’s four datasets. The repeated message is that software output and numerical summaries need graphs and interpretation.
**Maya:** The matrix supplement also helps us see what remains constant as the book moves onward. In a basic model, X has a column of ones and a column of x values, beta holds intercept and slope, and epsilon holds deviations. In a larger model, the same expression simply gains columns for additional explanatory variables. The foundational ideas here—conditional mean, fitted values, residuals, variability, and diagnostics—do not disappear; they scale up.
**Jordan:** It is also useful to separate statistical importance from practical completeness. In the Lincoln CAPM example, the slope has a t statistic of 5.64, so market return is an important determinant by the chapter’s test. Yet R squared is 35.4 percent, so the market leaves much of Lincoln’s return variability unexplained. A result can be statistically compelling without being a complete forecasting explanation. That distinction matters in risk management decisions.
**Maya:** Likewise, the lottery model predicts sales near 6,940 dollars at population 10,000, but the prediction interval is wide. The source’s lower mathematical bound is negative before it is truncated at zero, while the upper bound is about 14,641. That width is not a failure of arithmetic. It reflects natural variation in a future ZIP code’s lottery sales around the conditional mean. Predictions should communicate that uncertainty rather than hide it.
**Jordan:** So our final learner checklist is simple: label y and x clearly, plot them, compute correlation but do not mistake it for every type of relationship, fit least squares transparently, inspect R squared and residual scale, quantify slope precision, use t tests and intervals for the right target, and investigate unusual points in context. This chapter supplies those habits with enough algebra to understand them, before later chapters add more predictors and more complex models.
