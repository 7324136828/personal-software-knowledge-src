## 1.2 Fitting Data to a Normal Distribution
### Two parameters summarize a distribution

- Normal curve: f(y) = 1/(sigma*sqrt(2*pi)) * exp(-(y-mu)^2/2sigma^2)
- mu = location, sigma^2 = scale
- Example: MA bodily injury claims, n=272, mean 0.481 (log-thousands)
- Box plots and qq plots diagnose fit

**Speaker Notes:** Walk through the normal density formula, then show how the MA claims example applies mean/median/std dev/percentiles in practice.

## 1.3 Power Transforms
### Symmetrizing skewed data with Box-Cox

- Box-Cox family: y^(lambda) = (y^lambda-1)/lambda, or ln(y) when lambda=0
- Common lambda values: 1, 1/2, 0, -1
- Insurance claims are typically right-skewed
- Log transform (lambda=0) often symmetrizes best

**Speaker Notes:** Explain why analysts reach for logs specifically, and show the chi-square(2) simulation as a controlled illustration of the four transform choices.

**Sources:** Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex

## 1.4 Sampling and the Role of Normality
### Four Basic Sampling Assumptions

- E(y_i) = mu
- Var(y_i) = sigma^2
- {y_i} independent
- {y_i} normally distributed
- Normality (#4) often relaxed via CLT

**Speaker Notes:** Clarify that normality is the most dispensable of the four assumptions thanks to central limit theorems, unlike independence and the moment assumptions.

**Sources:** Regression Modeling with Actuarial and Financial Applications_013 - 1.tex

## 1.9 Technical Supplement — Central Limit Theorem
### Why normal approximations work

- CLT: sqrt(n)/sigma * (ybar-mu) -> standard normal as n grows
- Adequate for n=25 to 50 in most practical cases
- Edgeworth Approximation adds a skewness-correction term
- Regression coefficients are weighted averages, inheriting CLT normality

**Speaker Notes:** Tie the whole chapter together: transformations toward symmetry (Section 1.3) make the CLT/Edgeworth approximations more accurate for regression inference.
